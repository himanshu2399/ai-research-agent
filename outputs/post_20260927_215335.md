### Topic
Stop paying for orphaned PVCs: Pods die. Volumes don’t. Make “unused” a first-class signal and automate cleanup.

### Research Summary
- Problem: Kubernetes won’t auto-delete PersistentVolumeClaims (PVCs); it prioritizes data safety. When pods are deleted or teams move on, PVCs linger—and so do their bills. The hidden cost is both storage spend and engineer time spent proving a volume is safe to delete.
- Today’s pain: Teams rely on brittle scripts and manual cross-referencing of pods, mounts, and Slack archaeology to decide what’s “idle.”
- Shift: Treat “unused” as a first-class signal. If your platform exposes a native unused indicator on PVCs (or you add a controller that derives one from mounts/attachments), you can drive lifecycle policies with far less risk and toil.
- Playbook: Label PVCs on creation (owner, env, retentionDays), derive or read an “Unused=True” signal plus a timestamp (e.g., lastSeenMounted), and automate: delete in dev after N days, open a review in prod. Enforce labels with Kyverno/OPA and handle edge cases (pending pods vs terminated pods).

### LinkedIn Post
Stop paying rent on data you don’t use.

Pods die. Volumes don’t. Kubernetes won’t clean up your PVCs (by design), so the quiet bill is storage you forgot about—and the human time spent proving it’s safe to delete.

Most teams still guess: brittle scripts, grep pods, DM the team, “better not touch it.” That fear shows up as real money.

The shift: make “unused” a first-class signal.
- If your cluster exposes a native Unused indicator on PVCs (or you run a controller that derives it from mounts/VolumeAttachments), you now have two answers:
  1) Is this volume currently unused?
  2) Since when?

That’s enough to automate the boring, scary part:
- Label PVCs at creation: owner, env=dev|prod, retentionDays.
- Use Unused=True + a timestamp (lastSeenMounted/lastTransitionTime) to find idle volumes.
- Auto-delete dev PVCs idle >30 days. For prod, open a ticket/PR or quarantine first.
- Enforce labels with Kyverno/OPA so unowned PVCs can’t sneak in.

Hidden cost isn’t just capacity—it’s the hours engineers spend verifying “is this safe?” A trustworthy unused signal turns that into one field and one timestamp.

Edge cases to know:
- Terminated pods don’t count as usage; pending pods do (treat references as intent to use).
- Deleting a pod does not free the volume. Never assume symmetry.
- Scale-to-zero patterns (e.g., StatefulSets) need env-aware retention, not blanket deletes.

Forward look: treat PVCs like S3 with lifecycle rules. This is a low-risk, high-savings change most teams can ship in a week.

Would you auto-expire dev PVCs after 30 idle days? What retention policy would you defend in prod?
