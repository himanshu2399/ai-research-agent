### Topic
Separating Kueue admission from Pod scheduling to debug Pending Kubernetes Jobs

### Research Summary
- In Kueue-managed clusters, a Job marked Pending is often waiting on admission (queue policy, quota, flavor) rather than failing node placement by kube-scheduler.
- Practical debugging path: inspect the Workload’s admission conditions (e.g., QuotaReserved, Admitted), then follow linked objects: LocalQueue → ClusterQueue → ResourceFlavor/Cohort to find the bottleneck.
- Headlamp provides a clear, click-through view of these objects and conditions.
- Total resource requests scale with Job parallelism, commonly tripping ClusterQueue quota even when the pod template looks small.

Key insight: Treat “Pending” as a possible admission-capacity issue first; only debug node fit after Admitted=true.

### LinkedIn Post
Pending ≠ Scheduler.

If you’re running Kueue, “Pending” might not mean what you think.

Most folks jump straight to node fit, taints, and kube-scheduler logs. But with Kueue, many Pending Jobs are stuck earlier: the Workload hasn’t been admitted due to queue policy, quota, or flavor. Scheduling never even started.

Why this matters: if you treat every Pending as a placement problem, you burn time and miss the real constraint. MTTR goes up, and quota/policy blind spots stay hidden. Parallel jobs make this worse—your total request scales with job parallelism, so a small-looking template can exhaust a ClusterQueue fast.

Here’s the practical shift: separate admission from scheduling.
- Start at the Workload. Read its conditions (e.g., QuotaReserved, Admitted).
- If not admitted, follow the chain: LocalQueue → ClusterQueue → ResourceFlavor (and any cohort) to find the actual bottleneck.
- Only when Admitted=true should you spend cycles on node placement.

Tools like Headlamp make this smoother: open the Job’s Workload, check conditions, and click through the linked queue objects to see where capacity or policy is blocking.

Forward look: bake this flow into runbooks and dashboards. Treat “Pending” in Kueue as “possibly still queueing” until proven otherwise. You’ll cut debug time and surface the real issue—policy, quota, or flavor—not the scheduler.

What’s your default order of ops when a Kueue Job is Pending? Would you surface Admitted/QuotaReserved next to every Job in your cluster UI?

### Alternative Hooks
- Your kube-scheduler isn’t the villain behind your Pending Job—Kueue admission might be.
- In Kueue clusters, “Pending” often means “not admitted,” not “can’t find a node.”
- Stop tuning placement for a job that never entered scheduling.
